California Eviction Process in Orange County: From Notice to Unlawful Detainer to Sheriff Lockout

In California, a landlord can remove a tenant only through the court: serve the correct written notice, file an unlawful detainer case in the superior court of the county where the property is (in Orange County, the Superior Court of California, County of Orange), win a judgment, and have the sheriff enforce it. Changing the locks, shutting off utilities or removing the tenant's belongings is illegal, according to the California Attorney General. The California Courts self-help guide says evictions "can take 30 to 45 days or more" from when court papers are delivered; actual timing varies by case and cannot be promised.

Step 1: Serve the right written notice

NoticeUsed when
3-day notice to pay rent or quitThe tenant is behind on rent. It may demand only the exact rent owed, not late fees, utilities or damages. The 3 days exclude Saturdays, Sundays and court holidays.
3-day notice to perform covenants or quitA lease violation the tenant can fix.
3-day notice to quitSerious problems such as illegal activity or major damage. These 3 days include weekends and holidays.
30-day or 60-day noticeEnding a month-to-month tenancy: 30 days if the tenant has lived there less than a year, 60 days if a year or more. Usually requires just cause (below).
90-day noticeSection 8 subsidized tenancies.

Just cause. Under AB 1482 (Civil Code §1946.2), once a tenant has lived in a covered unit for 12 months, any termination must state a just cause. Nonpayment and lease breaches are at-fault causes; owner move-in, withdrawal from the market and substantial remodel are no-fault causes that require one month's rent in relocation assistance or a waived final month. For a curable violation, a notice to cure must come first. See what AB 1482 covers.

Local rules. The courts advise always checking local rules. In Orange County, Santa Ana has its own rent stabilization and just-cause ordinance (city page). A defective notice can lose the case.

Step 2: File the unlawful detainer case

If the notice period ends without payment or move-out, the landlord files in the Orange County Superior Court. The court's self-help pages name these Judicial Council forms: Summons (SUM-130), Complaint (UD-100) and Civil Case Cover Sheet (CM-010). Cases are eFiled and must list the justice center for the property's location; the court points to its local fee schedule for filing fees. The summons and complaint are then served on the tenant, and the response clock starts.

Step 3: The tenant's response

  • Served in person: generally 10 court days to respond.
  • Served by substituted service or posting and mailing: 10 calendar days plus 10 court days.

Most tenants who respond file an Answer (UD-105). Motions such as a demurrer or motion to quash can slow the case; the courts suggest getting legal help.

Step 4: Default or trial

No response: the landlord can request entry of default and a clerk's judgment for possession (forms the court lists include CIV-100, UD-110 and EJ-130). Answer filed: the landlord files a Request to Set Case for Trial (UD-150), and a judge hears both sides. Defenses such as habitability problems are where timing becomes hardest to predict.

Step 5: Judgment and the sheriff

After winning, the landlord has the clerk issue a Writ of Execution for possession (EJ-130) and takes it to the Orange County Sheriff with the sheriff's instructions and fees. The sheriff posts a notice; the tenant has 5 days to leave, after which the sheriff can remove them and lock them out. A money judgment for back rent is separate from actually collecting it.

Time and cost: what drives them

Whether the tenant responds, what defenses are raised, court calendars and sheriff scheduling all affect how long it takes. Costs include court filing fees, service of process, sheriff fees and attorney fees. They vary widely by case; get a written quote from an eviction attorney before you start.

What to do now

  1. Do not change locks, cut utilities or remove belongings.
  2. Gather the lease, rent ledger and written communications; calculate the exact rent owed.
  3. Check whether AB 1482 and any city ordinance apply.
  4. Talk to an eviction attorney before serving the notice; ask how fees work and whether trial is included.
  5. If you live overseas, decide who can sign documents and work with the attorney.

Example (hypothetical, not a real client)

A tenant in an Irvine condo owes two months' rent. The owner serves a 3-day notice to pay rent or quit on a Friday listing only the rent owed. Saturday and Sunday do not count, so the deadline runs into the next week (later if a court holiday falls in between). The tenant does not pay, and the owner's attorney files an unlawful detainer. The tenant files an Answer claiming the water heater was broken, so the case goes to trial. That path takes noticeably longer, and costs more, than one where the tenant never responds.
