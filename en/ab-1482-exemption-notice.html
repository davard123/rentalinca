AB 1482 Exemption Notice: The Exact Statutory Wording for Single-Family Homes and Condos

To exempt a single-family home or separately titled condo from AB 1482, the owner must not be a REIT, a corporation, or an LLC with a corporate member, and the tenant must receive the exact statement set out in Civil Code §1946.2(e)(8) and §1947.12(d)(5). For any tenancy that began or was renewed on or after July 1, 2020, that notice "must be provided in the rental agreement." Without it, this exemption is not available, and the rent cap and just-cause rules apply unless another exemption fits.

Last reviewed: November 12, 2026. General information, not legal or tax advice. For your specific situation, talk to a California attorney.

1. Who can use this exemption

Both conditions in §1946.2(e)(8) and §1947.12(d)(5) must be met:

  • The property is residential real property "alienable separate from the title to any other dwelling unit": a single-family home, or a condo or townhouse with its own title.
  • The owner is not a real estate investment trust, not a corporation, and not a limited liability company in which at least one member is a corporation (mobilehome park management is also excluded).
  • The tenant has been given written notice using the statutory statement below.

An individual owner, a family trust, or an LLC whose members are all individuals can generally use it. Before moving a rental into an LLC, check whether any member is a corporation.

Some properties are exempt for other reasons and do not need this notice, for example housing issued a certificate of occupancy within the previous 15 years (a rolling window) or a duplex where the owner lives in the other unit. The AB 1482 overview lists them.

2. The exact wording

The statute says the notice must use "the following statement." Copy it word for word:

“This property is not subject to the rent limits imposed by Section 1947.12 of the Civil Code and is not subject to the just cause requirements of Section 1946.2 of the Civil Code. This property meets the requirements of Sections 1947.12 (d)(5) and 1946.2 (e)(8) of the Civil Code and the owner is not any of the following: (1) a real estate investment trust, as defined by Section 856 of the Internal Revenue Code; (2) a corporation; or (3) a limited liability company in which at least one member is a corporation.”

The same text appears in both Civil Code §1946.2(e)(8)(B)(i) and §1947.12(d)(5)(B)(i). There is no official state form; the statute itself supplies the wording. The statute does not address translations; if you give the tenant one, keep the English statement exactly as written.

3. Where it has to go, and renewals

  • Tenancies started or renewed on or after July 1, 2020: the notice must be in the rental agreement itself (§1946.2(e)(8)(B)(iii)).
  • Tenancies that already existed before July 1, 2020: the notice may, but does not have to, be in the rental agreement; a separate written notice is allowed.
  • Every renewal: because a renewed lease is a tenancy "renewed on or after July 1, 2020," put the statement in each new or renewed lease.
  • Adding it at renewal: the statute says adding this provision to a new or renewed lease still counts as a "similar provision" for the just-cause rule about tenants who refuse to sign a renewal on similar terms (§1946.2(b)(1)(E)).

The law asks for the notice in the lease. It does not require a fresh copy with every rent increase.

4. What the exemption does not change

  • Rent increase notices still follow Civil Code §827: at least 30 days for 10% or less, 90 days for more than 10% (see rent increase limit).
  • City ordinances can still apply. A state exemption does not mean a local rent stabilization or just-cause ordinance is waived.
  • Security deposit limits, habitability, fair housing and anti-retaliation rules still apply.
  • Ending a tenancy still requires a proper notice and, if the tenant stays, a court case. See the eviction process page.

Common mistakes

  • Paraphrasing or shortening the statement, or using only a translation.
  • Telling the tenant verbally or in a text message instead of putting it in the lease.
  • Including it in the first lease but leaving it out of the renewal.
  • Owning through an LLC that has a corporate member and assuming the exemption still applies.

What to do

  1. Confirm how title is held and who the LLC members are, if any.
  2. Paste the statement verbatim into the lease, ideally in its own clearly labeled paragraph.
  3. Repeat it in every renewal or new lease.
  4. Keep a signed copy of each lease.
  5. If an earlier lease left it out, talk to a California attorney about how the earlier period is treated.

Example (hypothetical, not a real client)

An individual owns a 1990s condo in Irvine in her own name. Her first lease, signed in 2021, did not include the statement. At the 2026 renewal she adds the exact text, with a Chinese translation below it for the tenant. From the renewal forward the condo can rely on this exemption. Whether rent increases during the earlier period were capped is a question for an attorney if it is ever disputed.
