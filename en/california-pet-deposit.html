California Pet Deposit Rules: Does a Pet Deposit Count Toward the Security Deposit Limit?

Yes. In California a pet deposit is part of the security deposit: Civil Code §1950.5 covers any deposit or charge imposed at the start of a tenancy, "however denominated," so the pet deposit plus the regular deposit generally cannot exceed one month's rent (two months for qualifying small landlords). A landlord may not charge any pet deposit, pet fee or pet rent for an assistance animal, meaning a service animal or an emotional support animal, under California Civil Rights Department regulations (2 CCR §12185(d)).

Last reviewed: November 12, 2026. General information, not legal or tax advice. For your specific situation, talk to a California attorney.

1. How the cap works with a pet deposit

Civil Code §1950.5(b) defines "security" as any payment, fee, deposit or charge imposed at the beginning of the tenancy, and §1950.5(c)(1) says a landlord may not demand or receive security, "however denominated," above one month's rent, in addition to the first month's rent. Calling part of it a "pet deposit" does not move it outside the cap.

  • Most landlords: regular deposit + pet deposit ≤ one month's rent.
  • Small landlords: up to two months if the landlord is a natural person (including a family trust) or an LLC whose members are all natural persons, and owns no more than two residential rental properties with no more than four units offered for rent. This exception does not apply when the tenant is a service member (§1950.5(c)(5)).
  • Deposits collected before July 1, 2024 are not affected by the AB 12 cap (§1950.5(c)(6)).

Full details on the cap and refunds: California security deposit limit.

2. "Nonrefundable pet fees" and pet rent

A lease may not label any security as "nonrefundable" (§1950.5(n)). Because a one-time charge collected at move-in falls within the definition of security, a "nonrefundable pet fee" at move-in is risky. Get an attorney's view before using one.

Monthly pet rent is different: it is a recurring charge, not a payment made at the start of the tenancy, and no state statute specifically regulates it. A 2024 bill, AB 2216, would have limited pet charges but did not become law. If you charge pet rent, put the amount in the lease, and check local ordinances. For rent-capped units, an attorney can tell you how pet rent interacts with the cap.

3. Service animals and emotional support animals

Under California's fair housing regulations, an individual with an assistance animal "shall not be required to pay any pet fee, additional rent, or other additional fee," including an additional security deposit or liability insurance (2 CCR §12185(d)(2)). The Civil Rights Department's FAQ (August 2026) adds:

  • An emotional support animal (ESA) is not a pet.
  • A landlord can have a no-pets policy, but must allow an ESA as a reasonable accommodation when there is a disability-related need, unless a narrow exception applies.
  • No registration or certificate is required. If the disability or need is not obvious, the landlord may ask for reliable documentation; a certificate, ID card or vest alone generally is not enough (documentation from an individualized assessment by a licensed health care professional, including one done online, may be), and the landlord must let the person provide other reliable information rather than simply deny.
  • Breed, size and weight limits cannot be applied to ESAs.
  • The tenant can still be required to pay for damage the animal causes beyond ordinary wear and tear.

HUD changed its federal interpretation for ESAs on May 22, 2026. The CRD states that California's protections remain in full effect.

4. Pet damage at move-out

Damage beyond ordinary wear and tear can be deducted from the total deposit. The landlord must send an itemized statement within 21 days after the tenant moves out, with receipts or invoices for repairs (§1950.5(h)), and must take photos at move-in for tenancies beginning on or after July 1, 2025, and photos before and after repairs at move-out (§1950.5(g)).

Also note: a 2026 law, SB 1296, adds pet-policy disclosure rules for rental applications starting April 1, 2027. Read the chaptered text before relying on summaries.

What to do

  1. Decide your pet policy in writing before you advertise.
  2. Add up all move-in charges (deposit, pet deposit, any other one-time fee) and keep the total within the cap.
  3. Handle assistance animal requests as reasonable accommodation requests, not pet applications, and charge no pet deposit or pet rent for them.
  4. Photograph the unit at move-in and move-out.
  5. Apply the same written criteria to every applicant.

Example (hypothetical, not a real client)

A condo rents for $3,000 a month and the owner does not qualify as a small landlord. A $2,500 deposit plus a $500 pet deposit totals $3,000, which is within the cap. A $3,000 deposit plus a $500 pet deposit totals $3,500, which is over the cap. Another applicant provides reliable documentation of a disability-related need for an emotional support dog: the owner charges the same $3,000 deposit as for any tenant and no pet deposit or pet rent.
