What Is AB 1482? California's Tenant Protection Act: Rent Cap, Just Cause and Exemptions

AB 1482, the Tenant Protection Act of 2019, is the California law that caps rent increases and requires "just cause" to end most tenancies. Under Civil Code §1947.12, a covered landlord may not raise rent over any 12-month period by more than 5% plus the regional change in the cost of living (CPI), or 10%, whichever is lower. Under Civil Code §1946.2, once a tenant has lawfully lived in the unit for 12 months, the landlord needs a just cause, stated in the written notice, to end the tenancy. For Los Angeles and Orange County, the California Attorney General (oag.ca.gov/rentcaps) lists the cap at 8.7% for increases taking effect August 1, 2026 through July 31, 2027.

1. The rent cap: 5% + CPI, never more than 10%

The cap in Civil Code §1947.12(a) works like this:

  • Formula: 5% plus the percentage change in the cost of living, or 10%, whichever is lower.
  • Base rent: the lowest gross rent charged for the unit at any time in the 12 months before the increase takes effect. Discounts, concessions or credits the tenant accepted are excluded when finding that lowest rent.
  • Two steps at most: if the same tenant stays, rent can be raised in no more than two increments over 12 months, and the total still has to fit under the cap.
  • Which CPI: the CPI-U for the metro area where the property is (Los Angeles and Orange counties share one index). For increases effective on or after August 1, the change is April of the current year over April of the prior year.
  • New tenants: when no tenant from the prior tenancy remains, the owner may set the starting rent freely (§1947.12(b)). The cap applies to later increases.

The Attorney General publishes the resulting percentages. Southern California figures as listed on oag.ca.gov/rentcaps:

The cap is not the only rule. The landlord still has to give written notice under Civil Code §827: at least 30 days for an increase of 10% or less, at least 90 days for more than 10%. See the California rent increase limit page for notice details and local rent control.

2. Just cause after 12 months

Civil Code §1946.2(a) applies once a tenant has continuously and lawfully occupied the unit for 12 months. If another adult tenant is added before an existing tenant reaches 24 months, the rule applies only when all tenants have reached 12 months or at least one has reached 24 months.

At-fault just cause includes nonpayment of rent, a material breach of the lease, nuisance, waste, criminal activity on the property, unauthorized subletting, refusing the owner lawful entry, and similar grounds listed in §1946.2(b)(1). For a violation the tenant can fix, the owner must first give a notice to cure (§1946.2(c)).

No-fault just cause covers the owner or a qualifying family member moving in for at least 12 months, withdrawing the unit from the rental market, complying with a government order, and demolishing or substantially remodeling. For a no-fault termination the owner must either pay relocation assistance equal to one month's rent (within 15 calendar days of serving the notice) or waive the final month's rent (§1946.2(d)). Failing to comply strictly makes the termination notice void.

The eviction process page explains what happens if the tenant does not leave after a valid notice.

3. Which properties are exempt

The exemption lists in the two sections overlap but are not identical. Common ones for Southern California landlords:

  • Newer buildings: housing issued a certificate of occupancy within the previous 15 years. This is a rolling window, not a fixed year.
  • Single-family homes and separately titled condos not owned by a REIT, a corporation, or an LLC with at least one corporate member, and only if the tenant was given the statutory exemption notice. See the AB 1482 exemption notice page for the exact wording.
  • Owner-occupied duplex: two units in one structure where the owner lived in one unit at the start of the tenancy and still does (neither unit an ADU or JADU).
  • Deed-restricted affordable housing and school or university dormitories.
  • Just-cause only: a tenant who shares a kitchen or bathroom with the owner living there, and an owner-occupied single-family home renting no more than two units or bedrooms (§1946.2(e)).
  • Rent cap only: units already under a local rent control law that limits increases to less than the state cap (§1947.12(d)(3)).

4. Notices, local laws and the 2030 end date

Owners of covered properties must tell tenants that state law limits rent increases. For tenancies started or renewed on or after July 1, 2020, §1946.2(f) requires that statement in the lease, as an addendum, or as a written notice signed by the tenant.

Some cities have stricter rules. Los Angeles (RSO) and Santa Ana have their own rent stabilization ordinances, and a local ordinance more protective than state law generally controls. The Attorney General keeps a chart of local caps on the same rent caps page.

Both sections are currently written to remain in effect until January 1, 2030.

What to do as a landlord

  1. Decide whether the property is covered: check the certificate of occupancy date, who holds title, and whether the exemption notice is in the lease.
  2. Look up the current cap for your county on oag.ca.gov/rentcaps and check your city for local rent control.
  3. Find the lowest rent charged in the past 12 months and calculate the maximum before writing the notice.
  4. Serve a written notice with the right lead time (30 or 90 days).
  5. Before ending a tenancy of 12 months or more, identify the just cause and any relocation payment, and talk to an attorney.

Example (hypothetical, not a real client)

A 1990s condo in Irvine is owned by an LLC that has a corporation as a member, so the single-family exemption is not available. The lowest rent in the past 12 months was $3,400. For an increase effective September 1, 2026, the Orange County cap is 8.7%: $3,400 × 1.087 = $3,695.80, so the new rent can be at most $3,695.80. Because the increase is under 10%, the owner must deliver written notice at least 30 days ahead (plus 5 days if served by mail under CCP §1013, as §827 provides).
